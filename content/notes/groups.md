## Groups

A group is a set $G$ with an associative operation, an identity $e$, and inverses. Examples: $(\mathbb Z, +)$, the nonzero rationals under multiplication, the symmetric group $S_n$ of permutations of $n$ objects (order $n!$), and the dihedral group $D_n$ of symmetries of a regular $n$-gon (order $2n$). A group is abelian if $ab = ba$ for all $a, b$; $S_3$ is the smallest non-abelian group. The order of an element $g$ is the least $k \ge 1$ with $g^k = e$.

## Subgroups, cosets and Lagrange's theorem

A subgroup $H \le G$ is a subset that is itself a group under the same operation. The left cosets $gH$ partition $G$ into blocks of size $|H|$, so for finite $G$

$$|G| = [G:H]\,|H|.$$

This is Lagrange's theorem: the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. Corollaries: the order of every element divides $|G|$, $g^{|G|} = e$, and every group of prime order is cyclic. Fermat's little theorem is the case $G = (\mathbb Z/p)^\times$. The converse fails: $A_4$ has order $12$ but no subgroup of order $6$.

## Homomorphisms and quotient groups

A homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ satisfies $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$. Its kernel is a normal subgroup ($gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g$), and for a normal subgroup $N$ the cosets form the quotient group $G/N$. First isomorphism theorem: $G/\ker\phi \cong \operatorname{im}\phi$. Example: $\det: GL_n(\mathbb R) \to \mathbb R^\times$ has kernel $SL_n(\mathbb R)$, so $GL_n/SL_n \cong \mathbb R^\times$; and $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is the quotient of $\mathbb Z$ by the kernel of reduction mod $n$.

## Cyclic groups

A group is cyclic if it is generated by one element. Every cyclic group is isomorphic to $\mathbb Z$ or to $\mathbb Z/n$. Subgroups of cyclic groups are cyclic, and $\mathbb Z/n$ has exactly one subgroup of each order $d \mid n$. The generators of $\mathbb Z/n$ are the residues coprime to $n$, so there are $\varphi(n)$ of them. The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic; a generator of $(\mathbb Z/p)^\times$ is called a primitive root mod $p$.

## Rings and fields

A ring has addition (an abelian group) and an associative multiplication distributing over addition; a field is a commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. $\mathbb Z/n$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime. An ideal $I$ of a commutative ring $R$ gives a quotient ring $R/I$, which is a field exactly when $I$ is maximal. Example: $\mathbb R[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb C$, since $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb R$. Finite fields exist exactly for prime-power orders $p^k$.
